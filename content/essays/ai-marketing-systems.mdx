There are two kinds of marketing leaders right now.

The first kind is excited about AI because it means better copy, faster briefs, prettier decks. These are real gains. They're also the least interesting part of what's happening.

The second kind is thinking about AI as a structural shift in what marketing organisations can do with limited headcount — and what the humans in those organisations need to be good at when the AI handles everything else.

I'm in the second group. Here's what I've learned from actually building it.

---

## The wrong frame: AI as content factory

Most of the conversation about AI in marketing is about content generation. GPT writes the email. Claude writes the brief. Midjourney makes the visual. This is useful. It is not transformative.

Content has never been the bottleneck in marketing. Ideas are not scarce. **The bottleneck is always decisioning at scale**: which customer gets which message, at which moment, through which channel, with what offer, leading to which next step.

A large insurance company like Star Health has millions of customers at different lifecycle stages. Every day, thousands of customers are at a moment where the right message would move them — renew, upsell, engage, recover. And thousands of agents are making calls that are either perfectly timed or completely wasted, depending on whether the routing logic is right.

This is where AI changes the game. Not in the content. In the decisioning.

---

## The judgment layer vs. the execution layer

Here's a mental model I find useful: split the marketing system into two layers.

**The judgment layer** is where humans remain essential. What do we believe about this category? What is the right brand position? What is the customer actually experiencing, and does our communication reflect that honestly? What are we optimising for, and is that the right thing to optimise for? These are questions that require context, values, and the kind of understanding that comes from talking to customers and stakeholders — not from training data.

**The execution layer** is where AI should be doing most of the work. Given the judgment-layer decisions, which of the 1.2 million customers in state X should receive message Y today? What is the expected yield of dialling this segment versus that one? When in the day should the IVR trigger for a lapsed EMI customer in Tamil Nadu? What does the sequence look like for a customer who opened the WhatsApp message but didn't click?

When I talk about AI-enabled marketing systems, this is what I mean: **you are freeing human attention from the execution layer so it can be fully present in the judgment layer.**

---

## What this looks like in practice

At Star Health, we built what I'd describe as a learning system rather than a campaign system.

A campaign system has a start date and an end date. You design it, approve it, launch it, measure it, write a report. The campaign is the unit of work.

A learning system has a continuously running set of rules, triggers, sequences, and feedback loops. The unit of work is the improvement — a change to a segment definition, a new trigger condition, a message variant test, a routing rule update. Every week, the system is marginally smarter than the week before.

The AI in this system isn't generating the strategy. It's:
- Scoring leads for propensity to convert, to renew, to churn
- Routing inbound and outbound contacts to the right agent type at the right time
- Flagging QA issues in sales calls without requiring a human to listen to every call
- Predicting which customers are most likely to default on EMI, so recovery outreach can be proactive

The humans in this system are:
- Deciding which signals matter and why
- Interpreting anomalies in performance data that the model can't explain
- Making the calls that have brand and relationship consequences
- Evolving the system architecture as the business changes

## What breaks when you build it wrong

The most common failure mode: automating the wrong things.

I've seen marketing teams use sophisticated automation to send more irrelevant messages faster. The open rates are good (people are curious). The conversion rates are terrible (the message has nothing to do with where the customer actually is). The unsubscribe rate goes up. The brand trust goes down. The automation made the problem worse at scale.

The failure isn't the technology. It's the absence of a judgment layer. Someone automated without first answering: *What does this customer actually need right now? What would make them trust us more, not less?*

Another common failure: the data isn't clean. An automation that fires based on a lifecycle state that's two months out of date is worse than no automation. The customer gets a renewal reminder for a policy they already renewed. That's a brand problem, not an automation success.

The infrastructure for AI-enabled marketing is 40% technology and 60% data discipline, process design, and human judgment about what actually matters.

---

## The honest thing about where we are

We are early. The AI tools available today are genuinely impressive and genuinely limited in ways that matter.

They're impressive at pattern recognition at scale — finding which segments respond to which messages, optimising send times, scoring leads across millions of records.

They're limited at the things that require understanding context that wasn't in the training data. A sudden regulatory change in insurance pricing. A brand crisis that requires a pause in all automation. A cultural moment that makes a particular message tone-deaf overnight.

The marketers who will thrive in this environment are not the ones who understand AI best in the abstract. They're the ones who understand their category, their customers, and their business well enough to make the judgment calls that the AI can't make — and who understand AI well enough to build systems where the AI handles everything it can.

That's a different kind of intelligence. And it's exactly what this moment requires.
