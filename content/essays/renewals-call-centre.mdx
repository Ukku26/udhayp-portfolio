When I took on the renewals function at Star Health, I wasn't expecting it to change how I thought about marketing.

I'd spent most of my career on the brand and growth side — campaigns, content, digital. The call centre felt like a different world. 120 agents, inbound and outbound queues, dialling targets, QA scores, average handle time. Operations stuff. Not my domain.

Two years later, it's the thing that shaped my thinking about marketing more than almost anything else I've done.

Here's what I took away.

---

## 1. The funnel doesn't end where marketing thinks it ends

Most marketing teams measure themselves to the lead or to the first conversion. Acquisition numbers go up, job done. What happens after that is retention's problem, or ops' problem, or somebody else's problem.

Running a renewals call centre destroys that illusion very fast.

Every policy that lapses is a direct consequence of something that happened — or didn't happen — in the marketing and onboarding phase. The customer who bought under pressure from an aggressive agent. The one who didn't understand what they'd purchased. The one whose onboarding experience was so fragmented that they never really felt like a customer at all.

In a renewals operation, you're dealing with the downstream consequences of every upstream decision that was made without thinking about lifecycle. You feel it in the dialling yield. You hear it in the call recordings. You see it in the data: segments of customers who are almost impossible to renew not because the product is wrong, but because something in the acquisition or onboarding broke their trust before the relationship really started.

This changed how I think about marketing fundamentally. The question isn't just "how do we get more customers?" It's "what kind of customers are we getting, and are we setting them up to stay?"

---

## 2. At scale, data discipline is a marketing function

When you're dialling 10,000 customers a week, the quality of your data isn't an IT problem. It's a revenue problem.

An agent dialling a customer whose policy already renewed is a wasted call and a brand embarrassment. An agent dialling at the wrong time of day for a segment that only picks up in the evening is burning capacity. An agent using the wrong message for a customer who's already been contacted three times in the past two weeks is accelerating churn, not preventing it.

Every one of these failures is a data problem. And every data problem, at scale, has a significant cost.

What I learned is that the CRM data has to be treated as a living, operational asset — not a reporting tool. The lifecycle stage fields need to be accurate in real time, not at the end of the month. The contact history needs to be complete, including the digital touchpoints, not just the call records. The propensity scores need to be refreshed on a cadence that matches the decision-making speed of the team using them.

Marketing teams that think "data and analytics" is someone else's function will always be operating with one hand tied behind their back. The insight that a segment is under-performing is much less useful than the operational discipline to ensure the data feeding that segment's treatment is clean, current, and complete.

---

## 3. QA is a form of market research

Call centres run QA to measure compliance and performance. Agents are scored on whether they followed the script, disclosed the right things, handled objections within guidelines.

That's valuable. But it's not the most valuable thing a QA function can do.

The most valuable thing is treating every call recording as a customer insight. What objections are coming up repeatedly that the script doesn't address? What language is the customer using to describe their hesitation — and does it match the language we're using in our marketing? What questions are agents unable to answer that a customer reasonably expects them to know?

When I started listening to calls this way, I found a gap between how we talked about the product in our marketing and how customers understood it after buying. The features we led with in acquisition messaging were not the features customers cared about at renewal time. The language we used to describe coverage was technically accurate but not the language customers used to think about their own risk.

This is a product-marketing alignment problem, but you'd never find it by looking at the marketing numbers. You only find it by being close enough to the customer conversation to hear it.

QA, done right, is one of the most cost-effective forms of market research available to a large consumer brand. Most organisations treat it as a compliance exercise. That's a significant opportunity being left on the table.

---

## What it changed for me

I came into the renewals role thinking I was going temporarily off-track from my marketing career. I came out of it understanding that the best marketers I know have all spent time close to operations, close to revenue, close to the messy reality of what happens after the customer says yes.

The funnel is longer than marketing admits. The data discipline that makes a great renewals team is the same discipline that makes great marketing. And listening to customers — really listening, not through survey scores but through the actual words they say when they think they're just talking to an agent — is irreplaceable.

Marketing and operations are not separate functions. They're two teams trying to solve the same problem from different ends of the funnel.
